How U.S. businesses are actually using AI

An interactive look at where Artificial Intelligence has reached inside American companies — by industry, by firm size, and by the kind of work AI is doing. Drawn from the U.S. Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS) AI Supplement.

Data collected Nov 2025 – Feb 2026 · Source: U.S. Census Bureau, BTOS AI Supplement

The Census Bureau quietly runs one of the most useful surveys about AI that almost nobody reads. The Business Trends and Outlook Survey sample covers roughly 1.2 million U.S. employer businesses over a 12-week cycle, with about 200,000 cases fielded every two weeks. Twice a year it adds an AI supplement — thirteen questions about where AI is being deployed, what tasks are shifting from people to machines, and where Generative AI is showing up in everyday knowledge work.

This page lets you explore the three most interesting cuts of that data. Where AI Has Reached shows where AI sits inside the company — which of fifteen business functions have actually adopted it, and where firms plan to deploy it next. Substitution or Supplement? asks a foundational question: is AI substituting for employee tasks, or supplementing human work? The Task Audit looks specifically at Generative AI — when employees use it, which tasks does it touch?

Use the dropdowns to filter the whole national picture down to a single sector, or to firms of a particular size. The charts and the short interpretations beneath them update automatically.

The Headline Number

About 1 in 5 U.S. firms is using AI

Whichever way BTOS asks the question — current use, planned use, employee adoption, or Generative AI specifically — the answer lands between 17.9% and 22.6%. Roughly one fifth of the U.S. economy is in the AI game right now.

Reading this view

The "current" column tells you which functions AI has actually reached. The "planned" column estimates the all-firm share that expects to use AI in that function next. Because those values are now on the same denominator, the gap is a directional signal, not a certainty: a large positive gap suggests potential expansion, while a flat or negative gap may reflect slower growth, timing differences, or survey uncertainty.

Substitution or Supplement?

AI is mostly supplementing work, but substitution appears where AI use is deepest

Among firms that use AI, every industry sector reports more supplementation than substitution. Nationally, AI-using firms are about four times more likely to say AI supplements employee work than replaces employee tasks. But substitution is not absent: it shows up most in the same knowledge-heavy sectors where AI use is most mature.

The main pattern: depth of AI use

The main pattern is not replacement versus assistance. It is depth of AI use.

Every sector sits above the diagonal, which means supplementation is more common than substitution across the board. Transportation, Accommodation and Food Services, Other Services, Construction, and Agriculture are closest to the lower-left: they are not especially substitution-heavy, they are simply less AI-intensive overall.

The sectors highest on supplementation are also among the highest on substitution: Professional Services, Information, Finance, and Education. That suggests task replacement is emerging first where AI is already woven into knowledge work, not as a broad economy-wide labor shock.

The important caveat: this is about tasks, not jobs. BTOS does not show that AI is eliminating positions. It shows that some employee tasks are being handed to AI, while many more are being assisted by it.

The Task Audit

GenAI has found the paperwork layer

Among firms whose employees use GenAI, the dominant uses are writing, searching, and summarizing: tasks built around language and information. More relational or situated tasks are less prominent, but that does not mean they are permanently human-only.

What GenAI has not absorbed yet

The pattern is less "AI does everything" than "AI has found the paperwork layer." Writing and editing dominate, followed by searching for information and interpreting, analyzing, translating, or summarizing documents. GenAI is showing up first where work can be expressed as text, search, synthesis, or documentation.

Developing or researching new projects is not actually a low-use category: nationally it sits at 30.0%, which suggests GenAI is already part of ideation and early-stage project work. Customer support and tutoring are lower, but that should be read carefully. This question asks about employees using GenAI; customer-facing chatbots or embedded automation may show up elsewhere.

Coding is also easy to misread. It is only 12.7% nationally, but it jumps to 34.2% in Information and 23.7% in Professional Services. That makes coding a concentrated GenAI use case rather than a broad-economy one.

Firm size matters too. Large firms report much heavier use for document interpretation, paperwork, and data analysis. That hints that bigger companies may be embedding GenAI deeper into internal knowledge-processing workflows.

About the data

All figures on this page come from the U.S. Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS) — Artificial Intelligence Supplement. The BTOS sample covers roughly 1.2 million U.S. employer businesses over a 12-week cycle, with about 200,000 cases fielded every two weeks. The AI supplement adds thirteen questions about AI use. The data shown here covers responses collected between November 2025 and February 2026.

What "current" and "planned" mean

"Current use" refers to AI used in the last six months. "Planned use" refers to AI the firm intends to deploy in the next six months. In View A, planned function values are converted to an all-firm estimate by multiplying the function share among AI-planning firms by the share of firms in the selected slice that plan to use AI at all.

Suppressed values

The Census suppresses estimates with insufficient sample size. Where a value is missing in a small sector or firm-size slice, it appears as a gap in the chart rather than a zero.

Sector codes

Sectors use NAICS two-digit codes. "Multi-sector firms" are companies whose operations span more than one NAICS sector, which the Census reports as a separate category.
